# The Sundial Story ☀️💛

**A Mini Moments Matter Story**
*Series: Growing Minds (5–7+) — adaptable
Draft by: Content team — original unpublished draft, 2026*

---

"Before clocks," said Nana, "people looked at the sun."

Theo looked up at the sky.

"The sun tells the time?" he said.

"It does," said Nana. "Watch."

She put a paper plate in the sunny spot on the windowsill.

She pushed a pencil through the middle, standing up straight, so it cast a little shadow on the plate.

She drew a line along the shadow with a marker.

"This is where you are right now," she said. "One line. Look again in a little while."

"Will it move?" Theo asked.

"Let's see," said Nana.

So Theo went to play.

And when he came back, the shadow had moved.

It sat on the other side of the pencil, like it had turned while he wasn't looking.

"It MOVED," Theo said. "The shadow moved!"

"Did the shadow move," asked Nana, "or did the sun?"

Theo looked out the window.

He looked at the pencil. He looked at the shadow.

"The sun," he said slowly, "is on the other side of the sky now. So the shadow points the other way."

"That's what a sundial does," said Nana. "It catches the sun's path and turns it into time."

"Can we catch the whole day?" Theo asked.

Nana smiled.

"I think we can," she said. "If we're patient."

They marked the morning line.

They marked the midday line.

And after lunch, they checked the plate again — the shadow had crept around to a whole new side.

"Look!" Theo said. "It's afternoon already, and I didn't even feel it go."

"Time does that," said Nana. "It moves whether or not we're looking."

Theo crouched by the plate.

"People really used these?" he asked. "For real days?"

"For thousands of years," said Nana. "Before watches, before phone screens — people read the day off the sky, just like this."

Theo thought about that for a long moment.

"They must have known the days really well," he said. "To read them like a book."

"They did," said Nana. "Sunrise for waking. High sun for noon. Long shadows for evening. The whole world ran on a shadow turning."

Theo looked at his little plate.

"Do you think my shadow is still moving now?" he asked.

"Step outside," said Nana, "and check."

So they did — they stood on the path and watched their own shadows stretch toward the evening.

"Ours are pointing that way," Theo said, "the same as the plate's."

"Because you're a sundial too," said Nana. "Everyone is."

Theo looked at his shadow like it had just become famous.

They drew one last line on the plate when they came inside.

Four marks, in a row, showing the whole shining arc of their day.

"There," said Theo. "We caught the day."

You don't need a clock to know time is moving.

You need a plate, a pencil, a little patience — and a shadow willing to turn while you play.

## 💛 Mini Moment Reflection

After your sundial afternoon, sit together and wonder:

How far did the shadow move from your first line to your last?

Would the shadow have moved if we hadn't been watching — how do you know?

Why do you think people needed the sun to tell time before clocks?

What moved faster: the shadow, or your afternoon?

And then:

Where else can you see the sun's path turning — on walls, on floors, on the garden?

## 🎨 Let's Catch the Sun's Path

Ready to make a sundial? You'll need:

🟤 A paper plate
✏️ A pencil for the middle, pushed through by a grown-up
🖍️ A marker
⏰ A clock — just to check yourself (that's the fun part!)

Here's how:

1. Put the plate in a sunny spot that stays sunny all day.
2. Push the pencil through the centre, standing tall.
3. Find the pencil's shadow on the plate and draw a line along it. Write the time beside it.
4. Come back in an hour. Draw the new shadow. What do you notice?
5. Keep a mark for the whole day, then look at the size of the turn you captured.

A grown-up's "why" question:

"If the sun didn't move — if it hung still in one place — would the shadow move at all?"

That one is worth thinking about slowly.

## 🌱 As You Grow...

When you're little, a sundial is a pencil's shadow that creeps while you play.

As you grow, you might begin to wonder: Why does the earth's turning make the sun seem to move?

Then: Why are shadows longest at morning and evening, and shortest at noon?

Then: How did people across the world measure time before clocks came along?

Maybe one day you'll learn about the turning Earth, and seasons, and every clever way humans have kept track of days.

Maybe you'll discover that a paper-plate sundial was your first clock that ran on the whole sky.

The plate stays a plate. But the questions grow with you.

And that's what makes this moment worth remembering.

Because someday, you might stand on a path and watch your shadow stretch, and remember the afternoon you and Nana caught a whole day on a paper plate.

And maybe someone will tell you: "You learned to read the sky when you were growing."

Maybe that sundial was the beginning of a fascination with time and space.

Maybe it wasn't.

We don't have to know. We just have to watch what turns.

## 🌟 The Tiny Moment

It wasn't part of anyone's plan.

No one had scheduled an eclipse, or even a lesson.

It was simply... a paper plate, a pencil, and a child who asked how the sun tells time.

Someone pushed a pencil through the middle.

Someone drew a line and said, "Let's see."

Someone kept coming back all day to greet a shadow.

And for one afternoon, a whole family ran on sunlight, just the way people did for thousands of years.

That's the magic of growing up.

Time moves whether or not we watch.

But sometimes, we get to watch — and catch it turning.
